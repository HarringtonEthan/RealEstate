The one-sentence version

It’s a research assistant, not a robot salesman

Ethan set up a computer program that reads publicly available information every day — the same posts and records anyone could read in a browser — and picks out people in the Triangle who have clearly said they want to buy, sell, or move. It writes each one up as a card on your page with a score and a link to the original post. That’s all it does. It never contacts anyone, ever. You read the cards; you decide what, if anything, to do.

Public posts (Reddit and similar forums)

When someone posts publicly — “We’re relocating to Cary this fall, where should we look?” — they have chosen to say that to the whole internet. Reading public posts is no different, legally, from you scrolling Reddit yourself; the program just never sleeps and never misses one. It saves the link and a summary. It does not dig for the poster’s phone number, email, or identity — the way to respond is right there in the thread, as yourself.

The etiquette that keeps this clean: on forums, a genuinely helpful reply from a knowledgeable local agent is welcome; a sales pitch is not. Every card’s “suggested next step” is written with that in mind.

Source 2 of 3

Public county records (Wake County and neighbors)

Who owns a property, when it last sold, its tax value, and its building permits are public records under North Carolina law — the county publishes them on purpose, for everyone. Appraisers, title companies, investors, and agents look at them every day. The program reads the same pages and notices patterns worth a look, like a house owned 19 years by someone whose tax mail goes to Florida that just finished permit repairs.

Two lines it never crosses: it doesn’t label anyone “distressed,” and it doesn’t try to guess private life events (divorce, illness, money trouble). Records patterns are shown as research candidates only — cooler leads, clearly marked.

Source 3 of 3

Your MLS data (expired & withdrawn listings) — the part to understand best

This is the one source that is not public — it’s licensed to you as a Triangle MLS member. So the tool is built around a strict rule:

  • You log into the MLS yourself, like always, and export a file (expired/withdrawn listings) — about two minutes.
  • You drop that file into a folder, and the program reads your export, under your license, for your own prospecting — the classic, intended use of that data.
  • Expired listings only appear here after they’ve expired — the tool will never suggest approaching a seller who is currently listed with another agent (that’s the REALTOR® Code of Ethics line, and it’s built in).
  • The program never has your MLS password and never logs in on its own — automating an MLS login could violate your MLS agreement, so it simply can’t.
  • MLS-derived information is never published anywhere — it stays in your private lead list, never on a public page.

Your one homework item: mention to your broker-in-charge (or Triangle MLS) that you use your own exports for prospecting research. It’s the standard use of that data, but it’s your license, so a one-time “yes, that’s fine” is worth having.

The rules it was built around

The legal checklist, in plain English

  • Fair Housing: the system is physically incapable of sorting leads by race, religion, family status, national origin, disability, or anything like it — those fields don’t exist in it, and its instructions forbid guessing at them. Leads are judged only on what people said, where the property is, and timing.
  • Do-Not-Call / robocall / spam laws (TCPA, DNC, CAN-SPAM): those laws govern contacting people. This system contacts no one, so it can’t break them. When you choose to cold-call an expired or FSBO owner, the normal rules apply to you like always: check the Do-Not-Call registry first, identify yourself and your brokerage.
  • Website rules: it only reads sites that allow it. Zillow, Craigslist, Facebook, Nextdoor, and LinkedIn forbid automated collection — so it simply doesn’t touch them. If you see something in a private group yourself, you can add it to the list by hand; a person reading is always allowed.
  • Privacy: it only records what people published themselves, plus public records. No bought data, no “skip tracing,” no hunting for contact info.
  • NCREC advertising rules: any future outreach templates will carry your name and brokerage, and nothing sends without you approving it — each one, individually.

Honest fine print: this page is a careful explanation, not legal advice. The system was designed conservatively around these rules, and the two judgment calls that involve your license — MLS export use, and how you personally do outreach — are exactly the two things worth a quick word with your broker-in-charge.
